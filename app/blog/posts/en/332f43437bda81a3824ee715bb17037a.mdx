AI is innovatively transforming our lives. Developers, in particular, seem to be facing an environment where they work with new tools every week.

Yet, amidst this transformation, some things remain constant. We must generate sustainable code and write code that is easy for humans to read. Code that is difficult for us to understand will similarly be difficult for AI to comprehend.

If AI is not performing as expected, it seems we've reached a point where we should question existing brownfield issues and whether the team's development approach is flawed.

---

## Vibe Coding is Not Sustainable

It seems over a year has passed since the term "vibe coding" emerged. Initially, observing the outputs of vibe coding, I often joked with friends and colleagues, suggesting we "just go farming."

But what are the results? At present, I believe I can state with certainty: sustainable services cannot be launched through vibe coding.

If you explain to AI what kind of app to build, how to approach it, and the overall planning, and then instruct it to develop, it will never work perfectly on the first try. You'll find yourself repeatedly making corrections, even cursing, "This isn't right." Repeated modifications lengthen the context, gradually losing the prior context and continuously generating uncontrollable bugs. Only then do we examine the generated code and curse profusely.

Vibe coding cannot exist. It is safe to say this will be impossible even in the future. Simply saying "do it for me" cannot make AI 100% understand our desired approach from vast amounts of data. LLMs are essentially fill-in-the-blank models; there's no way they can simply execute the exact functionalities we desire.

So, how can developers achieve sustainable coding with AI? What is the role of developers in this era?

---

## The Role of Developers

I frequently hear that instead of direct development, our role is to constrain LLMs to produce the desired code and to program such an environment. Repeatedly issuing commands implies that it is the developer's role to configure skills and agents and to establish workflows.

The phase of doubting LLMs is behind us. As developers, we should aim to create an environment where LLMs can perform effectively.

### Harness Engineering

Numerous open-source harness environments, such as OMC, OMO, and OMX, have recently gained significant popularity. This is because, regardless of how advanced LLM models become, we still need to clearly articulate our requirements. We must minimize autonomous actions and reduce ambiguity.

These open-source harnesses restrict developer autonomy, forcibly reduce ambiguity through flows provided within the harness environment, and enable sub-agents to work in parallel on clearly delineated contexts. They yield favorable outcomes by enforcing constraints, even when developers do not manually construct each flow.

However, this harness environment is ultimately not tailored for our team. Generic harnesses, unaware of our team's conventions, design system, and deployment pipeline, presented limitations. We concluded that we need to configure a harness customized for our team or individual requirements.

## My Workflow

Listening to how others work, it seemed they were all encountering similar problems and adopting comparable approaches. I felt it was crucial to differentiate between tasks that can and cannot be delegated to AI, and to clearly define the stages requiring human intervention.

### Delegable Areas

Repetitive and clearly patterned tasks, such as code generation, refactoring, test writing, documentation, commit message generation, and PR creation, could be delegated to AI. With well-defined references, the results consistently met expectations.

### Non-Delegable Areas

Design decisions, requirement interpretation, final judgments in code reviews, and subjective assessments of user experience still required human input. Whether the results generated by AI "work" and whether they are "correct" were distinct issues.

### Required Steps

Once these two areas were delineated, the workflow steps naturally emerged.

1.  Process of reducing ambiguity
2.  Breaking down PRDs into the smallest units for delegation
3.  Reference routing for code generation according to correct references
4.  Code review stage
5.  Raising PRs targeting feature branches per PRD
6.  Reviewing (AI/Human)

### 1. Process of Reducing Ambiguity

To reduce ambiguity, I utilized the `/deep-interview` skill. When context is provided in a Socratic interview style, it iteratively asks and answers questions to derive PRD-level specifications. For both Bugs and Features, the initial step involves collecting relevant context (Jira tickets, Slack threads, Figma designs, Confluence specs) and feeding it into this skill.

### 2. Breaking Down PRDs into Minimal Units

For feature development, I employed the `/prd-gen` skill. It clarifies requirements based on `/deep-interview`, breaks them down into minimal PRD units, and even generates a dependency graph. The separated PRDs are executed by `/prd-exec` in dependency order, and the core idea was to run `/deep-interview` once more for each PRD to completely eliminate ambiguity.

### 3. Reference Routing

Initially, I consolidated all rules into a single [CLAUDE.md](http://claude.md/) file. UI component checklists, common rules, and framework settings were intermingled in one file, expanding to 341 lines. Claude reading all of this in every session resulted in significant token waste.

The solution was to reduce [CLAUDE.md](http://claude.md/) to serve as a routing table. Detailed rules were categorized and separated under `.claude/ref/`, and [CLAUDE.md](http://claude.md/) retained only matching conditions such as "read which ref when working on which file." For instance, modifying a `.tsx` file would trigger reading `ui-implementation.md`, and working on `*Form*.tsx` would additionally load `form-architecture.md`. This reduced the file to 137 lines, a 60% reduction, allowing Claude to selectively load only the necessary references.

```javascript
.claude/
├── CLAUDE.md (137 lines, OMC + routing table + common rules)
└── ref/
    ├── ui/
    │   ├── ui-implementation.md (Primitives, Props, CSS policies)
    │   └── figma-workflow.md (Figma→Code 4 steps)
    └── patterns/
        ├── form-architecture.md (Self-Contained Atomic Field)
        ├── feature-api.md (Mutation Hook Pattern)
        └── api-error-handling.md (ApiException Pattern)
```

There were instances of omissions with instructions alone. I implemented a dual safeguard by adding a `PreToolUse` hook to `settings.local.json`, which automatically injects a reminder to ensure the reference was read when modifying `.tsx` files.

However, it still occasionally gets overlooked, so I am experimenting with various methods.

### 4. Implementation and Self-Review

Once the PRD is finalized and references are configured, the `/ralph` skill executes an automated implementation and verification loop. This approach iterates through code writing → testing → modification until completion.

### 5~6. PR Creation and Review

Upon completion of implementation, `/create-pr` logically separates uncommitted changes, commits them, and then generates a GitHub PR. If working on the `main` branch, it automatically creates a feature branch. When a PR is raised, `/review-request` dispatches a review request message to Slack. Notion integration automatically handles task logging, difficulty logging, and 'Done' status updates on the task card within `/create-pr`.

### Key Skills in Use

| Skill             | Description                                                                                                                               |
| ----------------- | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| `/deep-interview` | Removes ambiguity from specs using a Socratic interview method. Iteratively asks and answers questions to derive PRD-level specifications. |
| `/ralph`          | Receives PRD as input and executes an automated implementation + verification loop. Repeats code writing → testing → modification until completion. |
| `/prd-gen`        | Clarifies requirements based on `/deep-interview`, separates into minimal PRD units + dependency graph. Saves to `.omc/specs/`.                 |
| `/prd-exec`       | Executes PRD files in dependency order. Per PRD: deep-interview → branch → implementation → self-review → PR → waits for user review.      |
| `/simplify`       | Performs self-review by checking the quality, reusability, and efficiency of changed code.                                                |
| `/create-pr`      | Logically separates uncommitted changes, commits them, and creates a GitHub PR. Automatically creates a feature branch if on the `main` branch. |
| `/review-request` | Collects current PR information and sends a review request message to Slack (including @mentions).                                        |
| Notion Integration | Automatically logs work, records difficulty, and marks as Done on Notion task cards within `/create-pr`.                                  |

### Notion-Based Task Management

Currently, my company rarely uses Jira, so I manage tasks using personal Notion. When starting a task, I always designated or created a new Notion card to link, and when raising a PR, I consistently summarized the content of the current working session and recorded it on the Notion card.

By doing this, over 130 Notion cards accumulated within two months. It was beneficial to have an automatic record of my daily activities over two months. Even without dedicating separate time for retrospectives, simply by scanning through the cards, I could recall which tasks I worked on, where I encountered blockers, and what decisions I made.

## Conclusion

No team can yet operate with 100% reliance on AI.
However, it is clear that the majority of tasks can be delegated to AI.

Recently, the team also initiated discussions on standardizing AI workflows under the title "Becoming SOTA-level Developers." Upon sharing our individual approaches, we found similar conclusions and agreed on directions such as separating the roles of CLAUDE.md, skills, and sub-agents, a Git strategy that differentiates between team-shared and personal settings, and data-driven skill verification. It is still under construction, and challenges such as the limitations of test code, token optimization, and how to demonstrate value to team members who do not use AI are ongoing.

We must clearly recognize unreliable aspects, delineate responsibilities between human and AI within the team, and aim to establish a robust workflow.

There's still a long way to go.

Challenges persist, including the limitations of test code, token optimization, ambiguous quantification, how to conduct efficient reviews, and how to demonstrate value to team members who do not utilize AI.

I anticipate posting updates as my thoughts evolve.
